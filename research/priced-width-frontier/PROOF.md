# Exact price and replication for separated batches

## 1. Contract

Use the chronological cuts of Mihir Srivastava's [#4507](https://github.com/CrocSwap/integer-mult-bounds/pull/4507) and the compatible finite menus of Nick Petalas's [#6658](https://github.com/CrocSwap/integer-mult-bounds/pull/6658). Cut $j$ has $q_j>0$ jobs per replica. At positive integer replication $a$ it must fill exactly $a q_j$ jobs using whole invocations of widths in a nonempty finite set $W_j$ of positive integers. Within that cut, every width has the required endpoint contract. Different cuts must each finish their own jobs. A program may be invoked any nonnegative integer number of times. Its declared positive rational price $c_{j,w}$ is paid **each time**. That price may contain a fixed invocation fee. The parent has productive width $Sa$, with $S$ a positive integer. Prices are additive across invocations and cuts.

For one cut suppress the index. Define $C(0)=0$ and let $C(n)$ be the minimum total price of counts $(k_w)$ with $\sum_w w k_w=n$, or $+\infty$ if no such counts exist. Define

$$
G(a)=\frac{\sum_j C_j(aq_j)}{Sa},\qquad
\lambda_j=\min_{w\in W_j}\frac{c_{j,w}}{w},\qquad
F=\frac{\sum_j q_j\lambda_j}{S}.
$$

Use the **total excess** $E(a)=\sum_j[C_j(aq_j)-\lambda_j aq_j]$, with $+\infty$ for an infeasible replication. The pair of objectives is to minimize $a$ and $G(a)$. Dominance requires weak improvement in both coordinates and strict improvement in at least one.

These hypotheses do not price sharing a setup across invocations, moving data across cuts, occupying a resource across their lifetimes, or operation-dependent discounts. A physical application requires those effects to be ruled out or charged by the stated contract.

## 2. Two residue certificates for one cut

Put $B=\{w:c_w=\lambda w\}$, $h=\gcd B$, $g=\gcd W$, and $e_w=c_w-\lambda w$. Thus $e_w\ge0$, with equality precisely on $B$. For a modulus $d$ equal to $h$ or to an anchor $b\in B$, put a directed edge from $r$ to $r+w\bmod d$ with lexicographic weight $(e_w,w)$. A path witnesses actual invocation counts.

Let $(\delta^{(d)}_r,A^{(d)}_r)$ be the least path label from zero to $r$. A shortest path can be taken simple because deleting a cycle decreases its weight in lexicographic order. In particular $A^{(d)}_r\le(d-1)\max W$. In the finite cyclic group the monoid generated by the widths is the subgroup they generate. Both moduli are divisible by $g$, so the reachable residues are exactly multiples of $g$. Certificate entries contain attained counts and every edge inequality $L_{r+w}\le L_r+(e_w,w)$. Along every path from $L_0=(0,0)$ these inequalities give a lower bound, and the attaining counts give equality. Dijkstra's algorithm with the lexicographic weights computes such labels.

Write $\delta_r=\delta^{(h)}_r$. For reachable $r$, $\delta_r=0$ exactly when $r=0$. Indeed, a zero-loss path uses only widths divisible by $h$. The empty path witnesses zero at zero.

A useful sufficient bound follows already from the smaller table. Divide the cheapest widths by $h$. Their gcd is one. With $m=\min(B/h)$, let $U_i$ be the least representable normalized size in residue $i\bmod m$. The Apéry test from #6658 is $z\ge U_{z\bmod m}$, since adding $m$ is always possible. The conductor $c_B=\max_i U_i-m+1$ is zero when $m=1$. For $n\equiv r\bmod h$ with $n\ge A^{(h)}_r+h c_B$, a cheapest-width completion of the attaining path realizes
$C(n)=\lambda n+\delta_r$. Every exact fill has at least that price. For example $W=\{1,5,6\}$ priced at $10,5,7$ has residue one modulo five with least loss one attained at size six. At size one the only fill costs ten.

The anchor table gives an **exact** cutoff for each residue, including early exceptions. Write $A_s=A^{(b)}_s$. For every reachable $s\bmod b$,

$$
\delta^{(b)}_s=\delta_{s\bmod h},\qquad
C(n)=\lambda n+\delta_{s\bmod h}
\ \Longleftrightarrow\ n\ge A_s
\quad(n\ge0,\ n\equiv s\bmod b).
$$

Projection from modulus $b$ to $h$ gives one direction for the losses. Conversely take a least-loss path modulo $h$. In $\mathbb Z/b\mathbb Z$, the cheapest widths generate exactly the multiples of $h$ since their gcd is $h$ and $b\in B$. Nonnegative counts suffice in a finite group. Append such widths to reach $s$ without increasing the loss. This proves the other direction. Any exact fill attaining that loss has size at least $A_s$ by the tie-break in the label. From $A_s$ on, append $(n-A_s)/b$ anchor invocations. An unreachable residue is infeasible at every size. Below its own cutoff a reachable residue can still have a feasible, more expensive fill. An exact amount recurrence $C(n)=\min_{w\le n}(c_w+C(n-w))$ resolves those early sizes, with the empty minimum equal to $+\infty$.

## 3. Least periodicity and its first valid replication

Return to all cuts. Define
$$
D=\operatorname{lcm}_j\frac{g_j}{\gcd(g_j,q_j)},\qquad
P=\operatorname{lcm}_j\frac{h_j}{\gcd(h_j,q_j)} .
$$
At $D\nmid a$ some cut is impossible at every size of that residue. At multiples of $D$ every cut has a reachable residue. The eventual prediction $\widehat E(a)$ is $+\infty$ in the first case and $\sum_j \delta_{j,aq_j\bmod h_j}$ in the second. Here $D\mid P$. From the single-cut result $E(a)$ eventually equals this prediction, which is $P$-periodic. Its zero set is exactly the multiples of $P$, since each finite summand is nonnegative and vanishes precisely at residue zero. Hence **$P$ is the least positive eventual period of $E$**. For any eventual period $T$, start at a sufficiently large multiple of $P$. Its zero must repeat $T$ later, so $P\mid T$. At feasible $a$, the normalized bill is $F+E(a)/(Sa)$. The period belongs to the excess.

The first replication of the periodic tail can also be found exactly. Choose $b_j\in B_j$ and use each corresponding anchor table $A_{j,s}$. Write $a=D t$ and $v_j=Dq_j$. For cut $j$, partition $t$ into the $k_j=b_j/\gcd(b_j,v_j)$ residues $u\bmod k_j$. Within that class $s=v_j u\bmod b_j$ is fixed and reachable. That cut has excess different from its predicted value precisely at the positive $t$ satisfying
$$
t\equiv u\bmod k_j,\qquad t\le M_{j,u}
 = \left\lfloor\frac{A_{j,s}-1}{v_j}\right\rfloor .
$$
The largest integer in this class below the bound is $t_{j,u}=M_{j,u}-((M_{j,u}-u)\bmod k_j)$, using the nonnegative remainder. Discard nonpositive values. Let $L$ be $D$ times the maximum remaining $t_{j,u}$, or zero if there are none.

Every finite cut loss is at least its prediction. On a multiple of $D$, one excess above its prediction, including infinity, forces a mismatch in the sum. At a nonmultiple of $D$ both values are already infinite. It follows that **$L+1$ is the earliest positive start of periodicity**. From that point all values equal the prediction. If $L>0$, $E(L)\ne E(L+P)$ because $L+P$ is past the last mismatch. This enumerates $\sum_j k_j$ residue classes without enumerating a global period or every amount up to the last exception.

For the menu $\{1,4,7\}$ priced $2,4,7$, the cheapest widths are four and seven. With anchor four the cutoff sizes in residues $0,1,2,3$ are $0,21,14,7$. The last exception at $q=1$ is 17. Add a separate one-job cut which can only invoke width four. All multiples of four can already be filled at minimum density by both cuts. All other replications are impossible, so the joint onset is one and its excess period is four.

More generally take $W=\{1,b,b+1\}$ priced $2,b,b+1$, with $b\ge2$. The cheapest widths are $b,b+1$. A minimum-loss fill of residue $s$ modulo $b$ needs at least $s$ copies of $b+1$, and exactly $s$ attain its smallest size $s(b+1)$. The sole cut has period one, first floor $b$ and exact onset $b(b-1)$. Adding a separate width-$b$-only cut priced at $b$ gives period $b$, the same first floor, and exact onset one. The gap between separate-cut and joint onsets is unbounded.

## 4. The finite Pareto frontier

Equality $G(a)=F$ holds precisely when every cut can use only cheapest widths. Section 2 gives a finite exact test for each such fill even before the periodic tail. There is a positive witness. Choose one $b_j\in B_j$ per cut and put
$$
U=\operatorname{lcm}_j\frac{b_j}{\gcd(b_j,q_j)} .
$$
At $U$ use only the chosen widths. The least floor replication $a_*$ is the first multiple of $P$ up to $U$ passing every cheapest-menu Apéry test. This is the least-replication problem of #6658 applied to the cheapest menus. It may occur earlier than $U$ through genuine within-cut mixing, or after $P$ through early gaps.

The upper witness can be tightened. Let $H=\max(1,\max_j\lceil h_j c_{B_j}/q_j\rceil)$. Any multiple of $P$ at least $H$ can be filled with cheapest widths by the conductor test. It suffices to search through $\min(U,P\lceil H/P\rceil)$.

For each feasible $a=1,\ldots,a_*$ compute $G(a)$ with the exact single-cut prices and keep a point only if it strictly improves every earlier feasible cost. These and only these points are nondominated. An earlier feasible tie dominates a later tie. Every later replication is dominated by $(a_*,F)$. Amount witnesses concatenate across the chronological cuts and retain each separate fill. The finite enumeration can grow with the arithmetic parameters and with the number of points to report.

## 5. Two families with a fixed fee per invocation

In both families an invocation of width $w$ costs $w+1$. Use $S=1$ and integer $N\ge2$.

**The exact #6658 family after pricing.** The two job counts are $N,N+1$, both with menu $\{N,N+1\}$. At replication one both cuts can be filled. For $1\le a\le N$ the first cut must use only width $N$. Indeed if it uses $v$ invocations of width $N+1$, then $v$ is divisible by $N$ and $v=N$ would require at least $N(N+1)>aN$ jobs. The second cut uses $a$ invocations of width $N+1$, the cheapest per job. The normalized bill is therefore $2N+3$ throughout $1,\ldots,N$. At $N+1$ both cuts use only the cheapest width. The exact frontier consists of $1$ and $N+1$, with final cost $(2N+1)(N+2)/(N+1)$. More copies buy only $1/(N+1)$ in normalized cost here.

**A family with arbitrarily many choices.** Take two one-job cuts with menus $\{1,N\}$ and $\{1,N+1\}$. For a menu $\{1,b\}$ the minimum invocation count at $a$ jobs is $\lfloor a/b\rfloor+(a\bmod b)$. Replacing $b$ singletons by one large invocation saves $b-1$ fees. Set $\alpha=(N-1)/N$ and $\beta=N/(N+1)$. Then
$$
G(a)=F+\frac{\alpha(a\bmod N)+\beta(a\bmod(N+1))}{a},
\qquad F=2+\frac1N+\frac1{N+1}.
$$
The first floor is at $N(N+1)$. Between successive resets of the two residues the gap equals $\alpha+\beta-K/a$ for a constant $K\ge0$. It cannot set a strict new low in the interior. For $a=k(N+1)$ with $1\le k<N$ the gap equals $\alpha/(N+1)$ throughout. At $a=kN$ with $1\le k\le N+1$ the gap is $1/k-1/(N+1)$, strictly decreasing. The points $1,N,N+1$ are successive strict records. A later $kN$ beats the $N+1$ record exactly when $k>N(N+1)/(2N-1)$. Thus the entire set of replication coordinates is
$$
\{1,N,N+1\}\ \cup\
\{kN:\lfloor N(N+1)/(2N-1)\rfloor < k\le N+1\}.
$$
There are $N+4-\lfloor N(N+1)/(2N-1)\rfloor$ points. The tie at $N=2,\ k=2$ is correctly excluded. Already at $N=12$ the ten coordinates are $1,12,13,84,96,108,120,132,144,156$. The closed form shows that even these four available programs and one universal unit fee allow an unbounded number of exact trade-offs.

## 6. Prior methods and scope of the result

The single-cut problem is an equality-constrained unbounded knapsack. Knapsack periodicity, residue methods and Apéry sets have a classical literature. Gilmore and Gomory studied knapsack functions, and Nan Zhu related equality-constrained knapsack periodicity to group knapsacks. The proofs above specialize that machinery to the common replication at separate chronological cuts. Their contribution here is the exact shared period and least onset, the corresponding normalized-cost frontier, and the two explicit fixed-fee families. [Gilmore and Gomory (1966)](https://doi.org/10.1287/opre.14.6.1045) · [Zhu (1998)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0166-218X(98)00061-4)

Nick Petalas's linear-price result is recovered when all widths at each cut have equal density. Then every feasible replication costs $F$ per productive unit, and the first feasible replication is the full frontier. The unconditional claims here concern the stated arithmetic model. Choosing actual compatible programs and measuring every cost needed to embed them in the selected multiplication construction remains a separate research task.
